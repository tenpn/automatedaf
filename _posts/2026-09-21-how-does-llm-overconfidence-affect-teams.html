A new pre-print paper shows that people were willing to put their faith in AI output, even when the AI being wrong would lose them money. What does this tell us about team dynamics in AI-forward teams?

AI advice suppresses people's willingness to say "I don't know", even when the advice is wrong and accuracy is incentivized by Marcoccia et al gave people a movie quiz. Money would be earned for a right answer, and taken away for an incorrect one. However participants could always click I Don't Know.

Some participants were given an option that was presented as from an AI search. These players were way more eager to hit the AI answer than Don't Know than the control group.

People confidently lost money because they over-trusted in AI output.

I'm not the first to say this but it is so important, as AI tools become more popular, to keep your head, trust and verify, and not get out over your skis

If you as a non-programmer can verify the output of your vibe tool, great, but that's only one use case. One thing I'm hearing about more and more is managers and non-programmers speculating about engineering tasks, "backed up" by LLM output

(Or, most egregiously, copy and pasting LLM output without being clear where it comes from.

In teams, open communication is key, and that comes from trust. If a non-expert opines incorrectly on a subject because they were given false confidence by LLMs, it throttles those communication channels. Maybe the team is trusting enough to push back, or maybe they'll just discount everything that non-expert says in the future

This post https://distantprovince.by/posts/its-rude-to-show-ai-output-... has some great pointers on post-AI etiquette in teams. If a stakeholder is going to ask ChatGPT for feedback to paste into slack, they should at least own that, but preferably trust the team to have done that kind of due diligence already.

We're going to see countless GDC post-mortems about this, I promise you.
