What I do can be summed up as "helping fix bugs earlier". Focusing on such a really simple metric helps raise quality and lower costs because it turns out having unfixed bugs in your codebase is bad in a bunch of subtle ways. It's easier to understand these costs by considering the ways that fixing bugs faster is better.

For instance, if it's not been long since a bug was created, the developer might still be in the same headspace. They don’t have to read code or documentation to get back up to speed, they can just dive in. They might even already know exactly where the issue is without debugging.

Bugs that have only just been created can also be less complicated to fix. The system might still be in active development, so fixing a fundamental bug with a quick refactor might not be as expensive as leaving it to later. There’s less time for other bugs and gameplay to become reliant on the behaviour of this bug, so there’s less knock-on effects.

When you fix bugs earlier, it becomes easier to prioritise and reason about your bugs. There’s less bureaucracy and pinging around the houses - it’s often very obvious who is best placed to address a bug because they were the ones most recently working on it. Think about how if the banana peels no longer caused spin outs in Mario Kart, it would fall to a producer to decide if this bug goes first to code or to art, with a 50/50 chance of getting it wrong and distracting someone unnecessarily. If this bug is found quickly, then the producer can rely on their knowledge that the artist was recently working on the banana collider.

At the project level, there are numerous benefits to fixing bugs as early as possible:

There’s less risk, because there’s less chance that unknown bugs are hiding behind known bugs. If there are any, they’ll be visible soon anyway, when the blocking bugs are fixed. It’s much easier to have confidence in the quality of your project because of this.

Developers can do faster playtesting, because the game is always much closer to shippable, and validating a build is much faster. This leads to quicker earlier feedback, at a point in the project when it can be properly handled. Now we’re not just making the game more stable, we’re making it more fun too.

Developers are more productive, not just because they’re spending less time on their own bugs, but because they are not blocked by other people’s bugs. All levels are playable, features are usable, tools don’t need awkward workarounds. More productive developers make better games, not just faster games, because they can stay in the zone for longer.

From all this, team morale is improved: everyone is gelling, momentum is high, people feel valued and productive and happy.

The conclusion here feels inescapable: that striving to fix bugs as soon as they appear has a dramatic impact on quality and productivity.
