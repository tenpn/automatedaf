It's too reductive to describe the kind of change I help happen as test automation. In reality, it's better described as "quality engineering" - a field which might include test automation where appropriate.

I thought today I'd show how I define quality engineering for my clients.

We have to start of course with defining quality, because that can be a confusing term for game developers. Are we just talking about bugs, or something else? And the answer is both - “quality” covers anything that might impact player satisfaction. If there’s a soft lock on level 3, that’s a quality issue. If there’s an animation issue when performing certain actions, that’s a quality issue. If the game is too short for the asking price, that’s a quality issue. If everything is great but it’s just a derivative boring game, that’s a quality issue.

Problems with quality will obviously hurt developer pride, but will have material impacts too. Instability can make it hard to do proper playtesting and slow down post-launch updates, a poor launch that impacts steam reviews can be really hard to recover from, and developer time can be wasted doing workarounds or repetitive manual testing.

Quality engineering is however not interested in raising quality in isolation - all other things being equal, we know how to do that. Just throw more money at the problem! - but interested in getting more quality for the same amount of money. Once you can do that, you have the freedom to either keep budgets the same to get more quality, or spend less to get the same amount of quality.
